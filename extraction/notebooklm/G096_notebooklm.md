## Resumo

This Master's thesis presents a multivocal literature review (MLR) on Developer Experience (DX), exploring its definitions, objects of study, research methods, influencing factors, and operational contexts across academic and grey literature [1, 3, 5]. Grounded in the foundational conceptual frameworks of Fagerholm & Münch (2013) and Fagerholm (2015), the thesis evaluates DX as an extension and derivative of User Experience (UX), explicitly differentiating the act of creating software from merely consuming software products [1, 6-8]. The work investigates how software developers experience software engineering both technically and socially, encompassing cognitive, affective, and conative perceptions within complex development environments [7, 9-11].

The study executed a structured MLR protocol adhering to guidelines by Garousi et al. (2019) and Kitchenham & Charters (2007) [12-14]. Database searches across IEEE Xplore, ACM, ScienceDirect, and Scopus alongside Google search engine queries were complemented by forward and backward snowballing iterations [14-17]. Out of 111 evaluated candidates, 59 primary sources were selected for synthesis—comprising 40 scientific articles and 19 grey literature sources, including industry blog posts, developer portals, and practitioner experience reports [18-20]. Data analysis combined quantitative aggregation with qualitative affinity diagramming (KJ method) to systematically compare academic research with practitioner perspectives [21, 22].

The results highlight key convergences and distinctions between academic and practitioner viewpoints [1, 23, 24]. Scientific literature adopts a holistic approach covering socio-emotional and organizational dimensions—such as developer happiness, team collaboration, shared understanding, and performance alignment [25-29]. Conversely, grey literature focuses predominantly on cognitive and pragmatic aspects, specifically API DX, developer portals, onboarding, tool usability, and business value creation [23, 30-33]. Both domains converge on defining DX as developers' thoughts, feelings, and perceptions regarding their work, marking an industry-wide shift toward greater empathy and respect for software developers [1, 34-37].

## Frameworks Mencionados

- **Developer Experience Conceptual Framework (Fagerholm & Münch, 2013)**: Discussed as a core theoretical foundation; divides DX into cognitive (perceptions of infrastructure), affective (feelings about work), and conative (perceived value of contribution) dimensions [7, 11, 38, 39].
- **Developer Experience Theoretical Framework (Fagerholm, 2015)**: Discussed as a foundational structural guide; models DX across social and technical environments, incorporating experience objects, influencers, formation, content (immediate, episodic, cumulative), and outcomes [7, 40-42].
- **Performance Alignment Work (PAW) Framework (Fagerholm et al., 2014, 2015)**: Discussed; explains how software developers experience continuous adaptation of team performance in Lean and Agile environments by aligning affective and conative factors [27, 29, 43].
- **User Experience (UX) Frameworks & ISO 9241-210 (DIS, 2009; Hassenzahl & Tractinsky, 2006)**: Discussed; provides the underlying HCI foundation from which DX evolved, contrasting user-centered models with process-product-centered development models [6, 44-46].
- **Programmer Experience (PX) Constructs (Morales et al., 2019; HPI, 2019)**: Discussed; contrasted with DX to clarify that PX focuses more narrowly on development artifacts and code interaction, whereas DX encompasses broader social, motivational, and contextual dimensions [47-49].
- *Note on DORA, SPACE, and DevEx Frameworks*: Modern frameworks such as **DORA** (Four Key Metrics), **SPACE** (2021), and the **DevEx Framework** (2023) are **NOT mentioned or discussed** in this source, as it was published in February 2020 [1].

## Metricas Mencionadas

- **Time to Hello World**: Evaluated in the context of API and programming language approachability; measures the elapsed time required for a developer to successfully write and run a basic "Hello World" program [30, 50, 51].
- **0 to 200**: Derived from HTTP status code 200 (OK); measures the minimal effort and time required for a developer to execute a successful API call during initial onboarding [30, 50, 51].
- **Productivity Level / Ratio (Output vs. Effort)**: Applied in mobile development platform studies (e.g., Nebeling et al., 2013) to measure developer output relative to effort expended [52].
- **Years of Experience / Number of Commits**: Discussed explicitly as **exclusion criteria metrics**; used in general literature to quantify developer seniority or activity levels rather than measuring subjective developer experience [47, 53].
- **Happiness / Unhappiness Indicators**: Qualitative and survey-based emotional measures (Graziotin et al., 2017a, 2017b, 2017c, 2018) evaluating developer affect and its consequences on problem-solving, code quality, and process performance [25, 29].
- **Production Metrics**: Referenced from Chatley (2019) as a quantitative mechanism to support and monitor developer experience using live production system metrics [54, 55].

## Insights para Praticantes

- **Empower Developers with Perceived Choice in Tooling**: Allowing developers to select their own IDEs, libraries, and tools significantly increases intrinsic motivation, flow state, and overall DX, whereas top-down tool imposition causes frustration [56-59].
- **Optimize Onboarding and Starting Experience**: For creators of APIs, libraries, and platforms, minimizing friction in initial setup (reducing *Time to Hello World* and achieving *0 to 200*) is essential for developer adoption and retention [30, 31, 50, 51].
- **Reduce Cognitive and Environmental Complexity**: Complex technical processes, ambiguous guidelines, and frequent interruptions degrade developer DX and hinder reaching a state of flow; simplifying infrastructure and providing clear documentation accelerates problem-solving [56, 57, 60, 61].
- **Foster Social Alignment and Shared Understanding**: Technical tools alone do not guarantee good DX; establishing agreed team values, transparent communication, mutual respect, and collaborative feedback loops directly improves team performance [26, 27, 29, 43, 62].
- **Treat Developers as Human Users ("Devs Are People Too")**: Avoid aggressive marketing tactics and sales pitches; developers prefer objective, fact-based documentation, clear code examples, transparent systems, and genuine empathy from tool providers [34, 37, 63, 64].
