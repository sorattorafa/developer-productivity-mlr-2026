## DETAIL (free text)
Developer Experience is extremely important for any undertaking involving software production, but the elements that affect this experience need to be improved so that the developer can achieve desirable requirements, such as productivity and results consistent with their condition. These elements can utilize strategies that allow for the improvement of the experience and minimize difficulties.
However, there are difficulties in measuring the level of experience of a developer, as it cannot be treated solely by the time it took to perform a given task. All the procedures necessary to reach the conclusion of their activities must be taken into account, in order to allow for advances in their productivity.
Generally, the evaluation of this experience is based on qualitative and quantitative metrics that range from the quality of the code produced to the ability to solve complex problems and effective collaboration within the team. In addition, the practice of self-reflection and constant communication with more experienced developers play a fundamental role.
These elements, when integrated, form a continuous learning ecosystem that transcends the simple delivery of code. The focus of DevEx (Developer Experience) is not only to improve productivity, but also to drive business performance through efficiency, product quality, and talent retention.
Some studies have revealed that companies with better work environments have experienced four to five times greater revenue growth than their competitors. Therefore, investing in optimizing the developer experience has gone from being a competitive differentiator to becoming a strategic necessity. To reach this level, organizations must implement key performance indicators (KPIs) that allow them to monitor not only technical results, but also team satisfaction and well-being.

On the other hand, factors that harm the developer experience, such as collaborative conflicts, frequent interruptions, tight deadlines, and constant changes in requirements, must be mitigated to preserve workflow and technical focus.

We can attribute the developer experience to three main dimensions, such as feedback loops, cognitive load, and flow state. These dimensions serve as pillars to identify and reduce the friction that hinders daily progress. In addition, the adoption of development tools and external APIs should prioritize usability and structural clarity, avoiding unnecessary overloads. Creating an organizational culture that values ​​focus time and autonomy is fundamental for developers to achieve a state of productive immersion, ensuring that the work environment promotes not only the delivery of high-quality code, but also the well-being and professional satisfaction of developers.

When optimized, feedback loops allow the developer to validate changes quickly, minimizing interruptions and the consequent frustrations caused by prolonged waits.
In turn, managing cognitive load should be a strategic priority, as the excessive mental processing required by complex systems or deficient documentation directly compromises efficiency and the ability to deliver value. By simplifying processes and reducing the complexity of tools, organizations can provide more effective support, allowing developers to focus on what really matters: creating innovative solutions.
The flow state is characterized by deep immersion and energized focus, fundamental to increasing productivity and promoting personal development. To foster this condition, it is essential to align motivational factors, such as recognition of work and autonomy, as well as offering adequate working conditions and a well-equipped technical environment.
In general, there are factors that can be easier to address to achieve the desired result, i.e., the continuous improvement of the developer experience. For example, improving the documentation of requirements for a system to be developed is more feasible than dealing with the personality style of a developer.

One of the studies analyzed (Greiler, Storey, and Noda) allows us to deduce that one of the possibilities for understanding and guiding improvements in the developer experience is the application of an actionable framework (DX Framework). It allows us to identify factors that impact the developer experience, clarifies the context of impacting factors, barriers that hinder the experience, and addresses the documentation of strategies and mechanisms to solve these barriers.

The authors used the DX Framework, with a methodology of semi-structured interviews with 21 developers from different categories (sectors, functions, and levels of experience). The questions were designed to extract relevant information, and during the analysis, the results were grouped into categories: experience, context, barriers, improvement strategies, and coping mechanisms.
A true understanding of the determining factors of the developer experience led to the conclusion that tasks during development need to be aligned with favorable conditions, clarity in defining the project and its added value, cultural collaboration involving the developer, their team, and company policies, in addition to maintaining the flow of task execution and promoting autonomy, maintaining a balance between requirements, production, and the developer's emotional state within the company - establishing conditions favorable to the continuity of the professional's activities.

Regarding the barriers, we can highlight low prioritization, difficulty in quantifying problems, lack of: visibility, awareness, support, ownership, incentives, and viable solutions, as well as undefined expectations, unclear processes, and organizational policies.

Regarding the strategies used to make improvements, we have the individual field: redefining tasks (job crafting), taking risks, speaking openly, local improvements, circumventing problems, imitating successful examples, and being pragmatic. In the collective field, teams can: build bridges between areas, create transparency, convince other members, make incremental changes, measure and track metrics, have a "driver" (improvement leader), and involve specialists.
Actions that may have a coping character involve: focusing on personal projects, validating negative experiences with colleagues, working overtime, ceasing to speak up, reducing engagement, "cheating the system" (e.g., inflating estimates), and as a last resort, quitting the job.
The developer experience impacts productivity, engagement, and talent retention. The DX Framework can be used by researchers and professionals to identify important factors, barriers, and improvement strategies. A three-step process is suggested: ask (make problems visible), plan (define responsibilities and resources), and act (incremental and continuous improvements).
The developer experience is highly personal and involves technical and organizational challenges. Improving it is fundamental to the success of software teams and organizations.


The article's main contribution is the creation of the DX Framework, a conceptual framework grounded in empirical data for understanding and improving the developer experience (DX).

The main contributions detailed in the text are:

• Comprehensive Definition of DX: the authors define the developer experience as "how developers think, feel, and value their work." This definition is based on the "trilogy of the mind," encompassing the cognitive (thinking), affective (feeling), and conative (motivation and value) dimensions.

• Identification of Actionable Factors: the study identifies several factors that impact DX, organized into four main categories:

• Development and Release: includes the health of the codebase, development environment, and automated testing.

• Product Management: involves clarity of goals, requirements, and the ability to deliver value to the business.

• Collaboration and Culture: highlights the importance of peer support, psychological safety, and code review processes.

• Flow and Fulfillment: focuses on autonomy, stimulating challenges, work-life balance, and career growth.

• Contextual Characteristics: the article reveals that the importance of each factor varies according to the context, such as the developer's seniority, individual expectations, objectives, and the company's maturity.

• Mapping Barriers and Strategies: the work identifies organizational obstacles (such as low prioritization and lack of ownership) and strategies that individuals and teams use to improve the DX (such as job crafting, "speaking openly," and creating transparency).

• Coping Mechanisms: describes how developers react when the experience does not improve, which includes reduced engagement, focusing on personal projects, and ultimately, leaving the job.

• Practical "Ask-Plan-Act" Process: proposes a three-step guide for organizations to systematically implement improvements: Ask (make problems visible), Plan (assign responsibilities and resources), and Act (implement continuous and incremental changes).
• Centrality of Psychological Safety: a crucial contribution is the evidence that psychological safety is fundamental, serving as a basis for all other factors of disability to be discussed and improved.

RQ1 — What important factors affect the developer experience? Answered in the Findings section, organized into 4 categories:

Development & Release: codebase health (quality/maintainability), development environment, tooling/automated testing
Product Management: clarity of objectives, requirements, delivering value to the business
Collaboration & Culture: peer support, psychological safety, code review
Flow & Fulfillment: autonomy, stimulating challenges, work-life balance, career growth

RQ2 — What contextual characteristics influence the importance of a factor? → "the importance of each factor varies according to context": developer seniority, individual expectations, objectives, and company maturity.

RQ3 — What barriers prevent improvement? → low prioritization, difficulty in quantifying problems, lack of visibility/awareness/support/ownership/incentives/viable solutions, undefined expectations, unclear processes/policies.

RQ4 — What strategies do developers/teams use to improve? → Individual: job crafting, speaking openly, local improvements, working around problems, being pragmatic. Collective: building bridges between areas, creating transparency, incremental changes, measuring and tracking metrics, having an improvement "driver," involving experts.
RQ5 — What coping mechanisms are used when things don't improve? → personal projects, validating negative experiences with colleagues, overtime, stopping talking, reducing engagement, "cheating the system" (inflating estimates), and, as a last resort, quitting.

"Psychological safety... is an important factor affecting developer experience" — serves as a basis for all other factors to be discussed/improved. And the synthesis of everything is the DX Framework + the Ask-Plan-Act process.
